# 2. Literature Review

## 2.1 Dugong ecology and regional context

Marsh et al. (2002, 2011) established the baseline understanding of dugong
ecology and the threats driving decline — habitat loss, bycatch, and vessel
strike foremost among them. This project weights observed habitat loss highest
precisely because the literature treats it as the most consequential and most
directly observable driver. The Marine Mammal Protected Areas Task Force's
*Southern Gulf* IMMA factsheet documents Abu Dhabi's population as the
second-largest globally, with the Marawah / Bu Tinah area at highest regional
density — the basis for this project's "dugong use" surface (Section 3.3). The
EAD's 2015 aerial dugong survey corroborates that pattern, and the Dugong &
Seagrass Conservation Project (UAE) documents land reclamation and dredging as
the leading local drivers of seagrass loss, and that Gulf meadows support only
three heat-tolerant seagrass species under summer sea-surface temperatures
regularly exceeding 36 °C — both incorporated directly into the threat weighting.

## 2.2 Habitat risk assessment methodology

The core framework — risk as **exposure × consequence**, high only where
valuable habitat *and* real pressure coincide — follows two established sources.
Halpern et al. (2008) established the cumulative human-impact mapping approach
behind this project's weighted threat overlay. Arkema et al. (2013) and the
InVEST Habitat Risk Assessment (Natural Capital Project) formalise the
exposure-times-consequence logic that `Risk = Value × Threat` implements,
including normalising each factor to a common 0–1 scale before combination. This
is why the index multiplies value by threat rather than summing threats alone —
which would flag high-pressure but seagrass-free water as "risky," a meaningless
output for conservation planning.

## 2.3 Regional and institutional context

The UNESCO Man and the Biosphere designation of the Marawah Reserve (2007), and
its Marine Protected Area status since 2001, make it the largest MPA in the
Arabian Gulf (4,255 km²) and the institutional context for the study area — and
the reason habitat inside the reserve, and especially the Bu Tinah no-take core,
carries elevated weight in the model.

## 2.4 Gap this project addresses

None of these sources combine seagrass classification, observed change, and
multi-factor threat data into a single, ranked, reproducible risk output for
dugong habitat in this region. They describe *where dugongs are*, *what
threatens them*, and *how to combine exposure and threat in general*; this
project applies that general framework with region-specific data and justified
weights to produce a specific, auditable output for Marawah / Bu Tinah.
